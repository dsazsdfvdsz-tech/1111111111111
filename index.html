The Ethics of AI in Healthcare — opening pages

When an algorithm misreads a chest X-ray, the consequences do not feel statistical. They feel personal — a name, a family, a missed cancer that should have been caught. This is the moral asymmetry at the heart of medical artificial intelligence: the technology promises to lift average accuracy across millions of patients, while imposing rare but devastating errors on a few. Whether that trade-off is acceptable depends not only on the numbers, but on who gets to weigh them.

The clinical case for AI is real. Studies in dermatology, radiology and cardiology consistently show machine-learning models matching or exceeding board-certified specialists on narrow diagnostic tasks (Topol, 2019). Hospitals adopting AI triage tools report measurable reductions in time-to-treatment for stroke and sepsis — conditions where every minute of delay is measured in dead tissue. To dismiss these gains as merely “promising” is to ignore the patients already alive because of them.

And yet the same papers that report these gains rarely survive contact with the wards. Models trained on Boston hospital data underperform on patients in São Paulo. A skin-lesion classifier built on lighter-skinned populations diagnoses melanoma less reliably on darker skin…
